\chapter{Inward Pointing Vectors in Manifolds with Corners}

Defining the notion of inward pointing vectors in manifolds with corners is substantially harder than in manifolds with boundary without corners. Let us first examine why this is. 

Here is a common definition for manifolds with boundary without corners:

\begin{defi}[\cite{Lee2006} p.\ 136]\label{defi:InwLee1}
    Let $M$ be a smooth manifold with boundary without corners. Let $p \in M$. Then $v \in \tanspace{p}{M} \setminus \tanspace{p}{\boundary M}$ is inward pointing if and only if there is an $\varepsilon > 0$ and a smooth curve $\gamma \colon [0, \varepsilon) \to M$ such that $\gamma (0) = p$ and $\gamma' (0) = v$.
\end{defi}

This definition cannot be used for manifolds with corners as in this case $\boundary M$ is not always a manifold and thus $\tanspace{p}{\boundary M}$ is not defined. 

The existence of the $\gamma \colon [0, \varepsilon) \to M$ by itself characterises whether a vector is inward pointing or tangent to the boundary, a notion which we will call \emph{realisable} or \emph{weakly inward pointing}. 

So for manifolds with corners we either need a completely new definition that intrinsically excludes the vectors tangent to the boundary or we need a definition of realisability and a way to exclude the tangent vectors. 

\section{Definitions intrinsically excluding vectors tangent to boundary}

We first tried the first option and considered these candidates for definitions which we either came up with ourselves or found in the literature: 

\begin{defi}[Our idea inspired by Definition \ref{defi:InwLee1}]
    Let $M$ be a smooth manifold with corners. 
    Let $p \in M$. Then $v \in \tanspace{p}{M}$ is inward pointing if and only if there is an $\varepsilon > 0$ and a smooth curve $\gamma \colon [0, \varepsilon) \to M$ such that $\gamma (0) = p$, $\gamma' (0) = v$ and $\gamma ((0, \varepsilon)) \subseteq \interior{M}$.
\end{defi}

Unfortunately, this definition doesn't exclude vectors tangent to the boundary: 

\begin{example}\label{ex:InwLee2}
    Take $M$ to be the upper half plane $\mathcal{H}$ in $\bR^2$ let $p$ be $(0, 0)$ and let $\gamma \colon [0, 1) \to \mathcal{H}, x \mapsto x^2$. 
    Then $\gamma'(0) = (1, 0)$ and thus by this definition $(1, 0)$ would be inward pointing even though it is clearly tangent to the boundary.
    See Figure \ref{fig:InwLee2} for an illustration of this example. 
\end{example}

Then we found a definition by \citeauthor{Melrose1996} of inward pointing vector fields. Here is an adapted version for individual vectors:  

\begin{defi}[adapted from \cite{Melrose1996} Definition 1.13.10]\label{defi:InwMelrose}
    Let $M$ be a smooth manifold with corners. Let $p \in M$. Then $v \in \tanspace{p}{M}$ is inward pointing if and only if $\direcderiv{v}f (p) \ge 0$ for every non-negative $f \in \smoothfunc{M}$ that is zero on $\boundary{M}$. 
\end{defi}

For some manifolds this definition includes vectors tangent to the boundary: 

\begin{example}
    Take $M$ to be the upper half plane $\mathcal{H}$ in $\bR^2$ and let $p$ be $(0, 0)$. 
    Let $f$ be any non-negative function in $\smoothfunc{\mathcal{H}}$ that is zero on $\boundary{\mathcal{H}}$. 
    Then $\direcderiv{(0, 1)}f (0, 0) = 0$. 
    Thus $(0, 1)$ is inward pointing by this definition even though it is clearly tangent to the boundary. 
\end{example}

Thus this definition is clearly intended to define what we call ``realisable''. 
To remedy this we came up with this definition: 

\begin{defi}[Our idea inspired by Definition \ref{defi:InwMelrose}]\label{defi:InwMelrose2}
    Let $M$ be a smooth manifold with corners. Let $p \in M$. Then $v \in \tanspace{p}{M}$ is inward pointing if and only if there is a non-negative $f \in \smoothfunc{M}$ that is zero on $\boundary{M}$ such that $\direcderiv{v}f (p) > 0$. 
\end{defi}

Note that this definition, in contrast to Definition \ref{defi:InwMelrose}, asks for the existence of such a function $f$.

Unfortunately, both of the above definitions do not work for manifolds with corners: 

\begin{example}
    Take $M$ to be the right upper quadrant $Q_I \subset \bR^2$ and let $p$ be $(0, 0)$. 
    Let $f$ be any non-negative function in $\smoothfunc{Q_I}$ that is zero on $\boundary{\mathcal{Q_I}}$. 
    Then clearly $\direcderiv{(0, 1)}f (0, 0) =\direcderiv{(1, 0)}f (0, 0) = 0$. 
    This means that the Jacobian is zero and since $f$ is smooth and thus differentiable at $(0, 0)$, this means that $\direcderiv{v}f (0, 0) = 0$ for all vectors $v$. 

    So in the case of Definition \ref{defi:InwMelrose}, this would mean that any vector was inward pointing, and in the case of Definition \ref{defi:InwMelrose2}, no vector would be inward pointing. 
\end{example}

This definition however does give the correct notion for smooth vector fields which was perhaps what \citeauthor{Melrose1996} intended. 

Lastly, here is another popular definition that falls into this category: 

\begin{defi}[E.g.\ \cite{Joyce2009} Definition 2.2]
    Let $M$ be a smooth manifold with corners. 
    Let $p \in M$ be a corner point of codimension $k$, i.e.\ let there be a chart $(U, \varphi)$ with $U \subseteq \bR^n_k = [0, \infty)^k \times \bR^{n - k}$ such that $0 \in U$ and $\varphi(U) = p$. 
    Then a vector $v \in \tanspace{p}{M}$ is inward pointing if and only if it is in $\derivat{\varphi}{0} ((0, \infty)^k \times \bR^{n - k})$ where $\derivat{\varphi}{0} \colon \bR^n = \tanspace{0}{\bR^n_k} \to \tanspace{p}{M}$.
\end{defi}

This definition is of course correct, however it depends on a choice of chart. 
In \mathlib definitions are generally preferred to not depend on choices. \todo{Why exactly? Reference?} Additionally, it only work for models with corners of the form $\bR^n_k$. For \mathlib we would need to characterise inward pointing vector in general model spaces. We will come back to this later. 

\section{Definitions through realisability}

After failing to find a usable definition intrinsically excluding vectors tangent to the boundary, we moved on to defining realisable vectors.
Where, as mentioned above, we want a realisable vector to be one that is either inward pointing or tangent to the boundary. 

Firstly, we could only find the following way to define inward pointing vectors from realisable ones: 

\begin{defi}[Inward Pointing, \cite{Amr2021}]
    Let $M$ be a smooth manifold with corners. Let $p \in M$. Then $v \in \tanspace{p}{M}$ is inward pointing if and only if it is in the interior of the set of realisable vectors. 
\end{defi}

Assuming one has already defined realisability as \lstinline|IsRealizable|, we can define an inward pointing vector in Lean as follows. 

\begin{lstlisting}
def IsInwardPointing {M H E : Type*} [TopologicalSpace H] [TopologicalSpace M]
    [ChartedSpace H M] [NormedAddCommGroup E] [NormedSpace ℝ E] 
    {I : ModelWithCorners ℝ E H} {p : M} (v : TangentSpace I p) : Prop :=
  v ∈ interior {v | IsRealizable v}
\end{lstlisting}

Now we can move on to finding a definition of realisability. We first note conditions for such a definition: 

\begin{rem}\label{rem:realcond}
    A sensible definition of realisability needs to fulfil these two conditions: 
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item The definition needs to be preserved by local diffeomorphisms (in particular it should be preserved by smooth charts). \label{rem:realcond1}
        \item The definition should reduce to a sensible notion on the model space that is easily verifiable in the cases of the typical examples (e.g.\ upper half space in $\bR^n$). \label{rem:realcond2}
    \end{enumerate}
\end{rem}

We first want to argue why the second point is valuable. 
First, since we are looking to define inward pointing vectors on manifolds in a way that is non-standard in differential geometry. 
Thus, we need to convince people that our definition is both correct and convenient.
Connecting to a concept that intuitively captures the right notion and verifying that it reduces to the correct notion for the typical examples aids here. 
Second, from a formalisation perspective it would also be helpful if this chosen notion already exists in \mathlib. 
This way, you would not be proposing to add a big chunk of non-standard material to \mathlib of which the usability is doubtful. 
Relating to a pre-existing concept can alleviate concerns in this direction. 

Now let us consider what this ``sensible notion'' in Remark \ref{rem:realcond2} should be. 
Motivated by discussions of cones in convex sets in \cite{Rockafellar1998}, we decided to explore cones as an option. 

We first need the following definition:

\begin{defi}[Contingent Tangent Cone, \cite{Aubin1990} after Definition 4.1.1]
    Let $E$ be a normed vector space, $K \subset E$ and $x \in \closure{E}$. 
    Then the \emph{contingent tangent cone} to $K$ at $x$ is the set of all $v \in E$ such that there is a sequence of scalars $c \colon \bN \to \bR_\ge$ and a sequence of vectors $d \colon \bN \to E$ such that 
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item $\lim_{n \to \infty} d_n = 0$, 
        \item $x + d_n \in K$ for all $n \in \bN$, and 
        \item $\lim_{n \to \infty} c_n \cdot d_n = v$.
    \end{enumerate}
    We denote this tangent cone by $\tancone{K}{x}$. 
\end{defi}

In \mathlib this is referred to as \lstinline|posTangentConeAt|.
In the following we will only consider contingent tangent cones to convex sets for which the contingent tangent cone agrees with other notions of tangent cones such as the Clarke tangent cone (\cite{Aubin1990} Proposition 4.2.1). \todo{Is convexity enough?}
Thus we will refer to it simply as the tangent cone. 

This captures exactly our notion of realisable: intuitively, the tangent cone $\tancone{K}{x}$ is composed of all vectors at $x$ that are either inward pointing or tangent to $K$. 
You can see examples of tangent cones in Figure \ref{fig:tancone}.

So we can take the ``sensible notion'' in Remark \ref{rem:realcond2} to mean that a vector $v$ should be inward pointing to a point $x$ in the model space if and only if $v$ is in the tangent cone at the point $x$ to the model space. 

Now let us consider possibilities for the definition of realisability.
The first was already provided to us in Definition \ref{defi:InwLee1}: 

\begin{defi}[\cite{Lee2006} p.\ 136]
    Let $M$ be a smooth manifold with corners. Let $p \in M$. Then $v \in \tanspace{p}{M}$ is \emph{realisable} if and only if there is an $\varepsilon > 0$ and a smooth curve $\gamma \colon [0, \varepsilon) \to M$ such that $\gamma (0) = p$ and $\gamma' (0) = v$.
\end{defi}

It is easy to show that this definition fulfils Remark \ref{rem:realcond1}. 
It also fulfils Remark \ref{rem:realcond2} but this is surprisingly hard to show for general model spaces $H$. 

In a model space without curvature, any vector that is inward pointing, describes a ray that lies in the set for a positive amount of time. 
Thus we can pick the curve $\gamma$ to run along that vector.

Finding such a curve easier for continuous curves (\cite{Rios2018}) or even for curves $\gamma \colon [0, \varepsilon) \to H$ that are just differentiable at $0$ (\cite{Rockafellar1998}). 
Requiring only minimal conditions for $\gamma$ eases defining such functions in Lean. 
In particular, if we were to require the curve $\gamma$ to be $C^k$ whenever $M$ is a $k$-manifold, then we would be providing a family of definitions instead of providing one uniform definition. 
So we arrive at the following definition of realisability based on \cite{Rockafellar1998}:

\begin{defi}[Realisability]\label{defi:realisability}
    Let $M$ be a smooth manifold with corners. Let $p \in M$. Then $v \in \tanspace{p}{M}$ is \emph{realisable} if and only if there is a function $\gamma \colon [0, \infty) \to M$ that is differentiable at $0$ and fulfils $\gamma (0) = p$ and $\gamma'(0) = v$.
\end{defi}

Another benefit of this definition is that by only imposing behaviour of the function at $p$, we don't need ask for the existence of a positive $\varepsilon$ such that $\gamma$ is defined on $[0, \varepsilon)$. 
Instead, we can ask for $\gamma$ to be defined on $[0, \infty)$ which simplifies the definition. 

In Lean this takes the following shape. 

\begin{lstlisting}
def IsRealizable {M H E : Type*} [TopologicalSpace H] [TopologicalSpace M] 
    [ChartedSpace H M] [NormedAddCommGroup E] [NormedSpace ℝ E]
    {I : ModelWithCorners ℝ E H} {p : M} (v : TangentSpace I p) : Prop :=
  ∃ (γ : ℝ → M), MDiffAt[Ici 0] γ 0 ∧ γ 0 = p ∧ mfderiv[Ici 0] γ (0 : ℝ) 1 = v
\end{lstlisting}

Note that \mathlib generally avoids using subtypes and instead prefers to use predicates on subsets. 
Thus this definition asks for a total $\gamma \colon \bR \to M$ but only imposes conditions on its behaviour at $p$ from the right side.

In a normed space Definition \ref{defi:realisability} reduces to the following definition which we will call by the name derivability instead of realisability in accordance with \cite{Rockafellar1998}.

\todo{cite precise definition number}
\begin{defi}[Derivability, \cite{Rockafellar1998}]\label{defi:derivability}
Let $E$ be a normed space over $\bR$, let $C \subseteq E$ be a set and let $p \in E$ be a point. 
We call a vector $v$ \emph{derivable} in $C$ at $p$ if there is and $\varepsilon > 0$ and a function $\gamma \colon [0, \varepsilon) \to E$ that is differentiable at $0$ with $\gamma ([0, \varepsilon)) \subseteq C$, $\gamma (0) = p$ and $\gamma' (0) = v$. 
The set of all derivable vectors in $C$ at $p$ is called the \emph{derivable cone} to $C$ at $p$. 
\end{defi}

In Lean this definition takes the following form: 

\begin{lstlisting}
def derivableWithinAt {E : Type*} [NormedAddCommGroup E] [NormedSpace ℝ E] 
    (s : Set E) (p : E) (v : E) : Prop :=
    ∃ (γ : ℝ → E), DifferentiableWithinAt ℝ γ (Ici 0) 0 ∧ γ 0 = p ∧
      derivWithin γ (Ici 0) (0 : ℝ) = v ∧ ∀ᶠ (x : ℝ) in 𝓝[≥] 0, γ x ∈ s
\end{lstlisting}

Again, the function $\gamma$ is taken to be total and instead we use predicates on the set $[0, \infty)$. 
Additionally, we use the weaker condition that $\gamma (x)$ eventually lies in $s$ as $x$ approaches $0$ from the right. 
This allows us to drop the $\varepsilon$ from Definition \ref{defi:derivability}.

\section{Preservation under charts}

First, we want to show that realisability is preserved under charts. 
We show this more generally for partial diffeomorphisms. 
\todo[inline]{Define partial diffeomorphisms in prerequisites section.}

\begin{lem}\label{lem:invariantRealisable}
Let $M$ and $M'$ be two $n$-manifolds for $n > 0$, potentially over different model spaces. 
Take $p \in M$ and $v \in \tanspace{p}{M}$. 
Let $f \colon M \to M'$ be a partial $n$-diffeomorphism such that $p$ lies in its source.
Then $v$ is realisable at $p$ if and only if $\derivat{f}{p}(v)$ is realisable at $f (p)$.
\end{lem}

\begin{proof}
We first prove the forward direction. 
By assumption there is $\gamma \colon [0, \infty) \to M$ that is differentiable at $0$ with $\gamma (0) = p$ and $\gamma'(0) = v$. 
We show that $f \circ \gamma$ realises $\derivat{f}{p}(v)$. 
It is clear that $f \circ \gamma$ is differentiable at $0$ and $f (\gamma (0)) = f (p)$. 
Additionally,
\begin{equation*}
(f \circ \gamma)' (0) = (\derivat{f}{\gamma (0)} \circ \gamma') (0) = \derivat{f}{p}(v).
\end{equation*}

For the backward direction, we can use
\begin{equation*}
v = \derivat{\inv{f}}{f(p)}(\derivat{f}{p}(v))
\end{equation*}
and then apply the forward direction. 
\end{proof}

\begin{cor}\label{cor:invariantUnderChartsRealisable}
Let $M$ be an $n$-manifold with $n > 0$ over the model space $H$. Let $p \in M$, $v \in \tanspace{p}{M}$ and $(U, \varphi)$ a chart at $p$. 
Then $v$ is realisable at $p$ on $M$ if and only if $\derivat{\varphi}{p}(v)$ is realisable at $\varphi(p)$ on $H$. 
\end{cor}

\begin{proof}
The chart $(U, \varphi)$ is a local diffeomorphism at $p$. This means that we can pick a partial diffeomorphism $f \colon M \to H$ such that $p$ lies in its source and $\varphi$ and $f$ agree on the source. Then we can apply the above lemma to obtain that $v$ is realisable at $p$ if and only if $\derivat{f}{p}(v) = \derivat{\varphi}{p}(v)$ is realisable at $f(p) = \varphi(p)$.
\end{proof}

Now we want to connect realisability on manifold with derivability in normed spaces. 

\begin{lem}\label{lem:reduceNormedSpaceDerivable}
Let $M$ be an $n$-manifold with $n > 0$ with the model with corners $I \colon H \to E$.  
Let $p \in M$, $v \in \tanspace{p}{M}$ and $(U, \varphi)$ a chart at $p$. 
Then $v$ is realisable at $p$ if and only if $\derivat{(I \circ \varphi)}{p}(v)$ is derivable within $I(H)$ at $I(\varphi(p))$.
\end{lem}

\begin{proof}
By the above corollary, it suffices to show the following: if we take $p \in H$ and $v \in \tanspace{p}{H}$ then $v$ is realisable at $p$ if and only if $\derivat{I}{p}(v)$ is derivable within $I(H)$ at $I(p)$. 

Let us first prove the forward direction. By assumption, there is a $\gamma \colon [0, \infty) \to H$ such that $\gamma$ is differentiable with $\gamma'(0) = v$ and $\gamma (0) = p$. We want to show that
\begin{equation*}
I \circ \restrict{\gamma}{[0, 1)} \colon [0, 1) \to E
\end{equation*}

witnesses that $\derivat{I}{p}(v)$ is derivable at $I(p)$ within $I(H)$. 
Clearly, $I \circ \restrict{\gamma}{[0, 1)}$ is differentiable at $0$, $I (\restrict{\gamma}{[0, 1)}(0)) = I(p)$ and $I(\restrict{\gamma}{[0, 1)}([0, 1))) \subseteq I(H)$. 
Lastly, 
\begin{equation*}
\derivat{\left(I \circ \restrict{\gamma}{[0, 1)}\right)}{p}(0) = (\derivat{I}{\gamma (0)} \circ \gamma') (0) = \derivat{I}{p}(v)
\end{equation*}

For the backward direction, take $\varepsilon > 0$ and $\gamma \colon [0, \varepsilon) \to E$ that is differentiable at $0$ and fulfils $\gamma (0) = I(p)$, $\gamma' (0) = \derivat{I}{p}(v)$ and $\gamma([0, \infty)) \subseteq I(H)$. 
We now want to show that
\begin{equation*}
\mu \colon [0, \varepsilon) \to H, x \mapsto \begin{cases}
    \inv{I}(\gamma (x)) & x < \varepsilon \\
    \inv{I}(\gamma (0)) & x \ge \varepsilon \\
\end{cases} 
\end{equation*}
realises $v$. 
It is easy to see that $\mu$ is differentiable at $0$ and that $\mu (0) = \inv{I}(\gamma (0)) = \inv{I}(I(p)) = p$. 
It is left to show that $\mu'(0) = v$. Since $\derivat{I}{p} \colon \tanspace{p}{H} \to E$ is injective, we can instead show that $\derivat{I}{p}(\mu' (0)) = \derivat{I}{p}$. 
We get
\begin{equation*}
\begin{split}
\derivat{I}{p}(\mu' (0)) &= \derivat{I}{p}((\inv{I} \circ \gamma)' (0)) \\
&= (\derivat{I}{p} \circ \derivat{\inv{I}}{I(p)} \circ \gamma') (0) \\
& = (\derivat{(I \circ \inv{I})}{I(p)} \circ \gamma') (0) \\
& = \gamma' (0) \\
& = \derivat{I}{p}(v).
\end{split}
\end{equation*}
\end{proof}

Now let us prove these same things about inward pointing vectors: 

\begin{lem}
Let $M$ and $M'$ be two $n$-manifolds for $n > 0$, potentially over different model spaces. 
Take $p \in M$ and $v \in \tanspace{p}{M}$. 
Let $f \colon M \to M'$ be a partial $n$-diffeomorphism such that $p$ lies in its source.
Then $v$ is inward pointing at $p$ if and only if $\derivat{f}{p}(v)$ is inward pointing at $f (p)$.
\end{lem}

\begin{proof}
Since $\derivat{f}{p}$ is bijective, it suffices to show that 
\begin{equation*}
\interior{\{v \in \tanspace{f(p)}{M'} \mid v \text{ is realisable}\}} = 
\derivat{f}{p}(\interior{\{v \in \tanspace{p}{M} \mid v \text{ is realisable} \}}). 
\end{equation*}
Again, because $\derivat{f}{p}$ is a homeomorphism, it suffices to show that 
\begin{equation*}
\{v \in \tanspace{f(p)}{M'} \mid v \text{ is realisable}\} = 
\derivat{f}{p}(\{v \in \tanspace{p}{M} \mid v \text{ is realisable} \}). 
\end{equation*}

By Lemma \ref{lem:invariantRealisable}, $v \in \tanspace{f(p)}{M'}$ is realisable if and only if $\derivat{\inv{f}}{f(p)}(v) \in \tanspace{p}{M}$ is realisable. 
Since we have
\begin{equation*}
\derivat{\inv{f}}{f(p)} = \inv{(\derivat{f}{p})},
\end{equation*}

this is equivalent to 
\begin{equation*}
v \in \derivat{f}{p}(\{v \in \tanspace{p}{M} \mid v \text{ is realisable} \}).
\end{equation*}
\end{proof}

\section{Equivalence with tangent cone on model space}

We now need to show that this definition of realisability does indeed equal our desired notion of being in the tangent cone. 

For this we need to describe different convex cones. 
Let us first define what a convex cone actually is: 
\begin{defi}\label{defi:convexCone}
Let $R$ be an ordered ring and $M$ a module over $R$. 
A set $s \subseteq M$ is a \emph{convex cone} if 
\begin{enumerate}
    \item for all $c \in R$ with $c > 0$ and $x \in s$, we have $c \cdot x \in s$, and 
    \item for all $x, y \in s$ we get $x + y \in s$.  \label{point:convecConeAdd}
\end{enumerate}
\end{defi}

Under stricter conditions there is an alternative way to show that a set in a convex cone:

\begin{lem}
Let $R$ be a field with a linear order. 
Additionally assume that $R$ is strictly ordered field, i.e.\ a field in which addition and multiplication by a positive element are strictly monotone. 
Let $M$ be a module over $R$. 
Let $s \subseteq M$ be a set. 
If 
\begin{enumerate}
    \item $s$ is convex and 
    \item for all $c \in R$ with $c > 0$ and $x \in s$ we have $c \cdot x \in s$ \label{point:convexConeOfConvexSmul}
\end{enumerate}
then $s$ is a convex cone. 
\end{lem}
\begin{proof}
We only need to show Property \ref{point:convecConeAdd} of Definition \ref{defi:convexCone}.
Let $x, y \in s$. We need to show $x + y \in s$. 
We have 
\begin{equation*}
x + y = 2 \cdot \Bigl(\frac{1}{2} \cdot x + \frac{1}{2} \cdot y\Bigr).   
\end{equation*}
By Property (i), $\frac{1}{2} \cdot x + \frac{1}{2} \cdot y \in s$ and then by Property (ii) $x + y \in s$.  
\end{proof}

For the rest of this section let $E$ be a normed space over $\bR$, let $C \subseteq E$ be a set and let $p \in E$ be a point. 

First we need to show the following lemma: 

\begin{lem}
Let $C$ be convex.
Then the derivable cone to $C$ at $p$ is a convex cone.
\end{lem}

\begin{proof}
We want to use the above lemma. 
We first show that the derivable cone is convex. 
Thus let $v_1$ and $v_2$ be derivable vectors in $C$ at $p$.
Then there are $\varepsilon_1, \varepsilon_2 > 0$ and $\gamma_1 \colon [0, \varepsilon_1) \to E$ and $\gamma_2 \colon [0, \varepsilon_2) \to E$ such that $\gamma_1$ and $\gamma_2$ are differentiable at $0$, we get $\gamma_1([0, \varepsilon_1)), \gamma_2([0, \varepsilon_2)) \subseteq C$, $\gamma_1(0) = \gamma_2(0) = p$ and $\gamma_1'(0)=v_1$ and $\gamma_2'(0) = v_2$.
Let $a, b \in [0, 1]$ such that $a + b = 1$. 
We need to show that $a \cdot v_1 + b \cdot v_2$ is derivable. 
We set $\varepsilon \coloneq \min \varepsilon_1 \varepsilon_2$ and define $\gamma \colon [0, \varepsilon) \to E, x \mapsto a \cdot \gamma_1(x) + b \cdot \gamma_2(x)$.  
Then we know that $\gamma$ is differentiable at $0$ and we get $\gamma (0) = a \cdot p + b \cdot p = p$. Additionally 
\begin{equation*}
\gamma'(0) = a \cdot \gamma_1'(0) + b \cdot \gamma_2'(0) = a \cdot v_1 + b \cdot v_2
\end{equation*}
and because $C$ is convex, $\gamma([0, \varepsilon)) \subseteq C$. 
Thus $a \cdot v_1 + b \cdot v_2$ is derivable. 

Now we show Property \ref{point:convexConeOfConvexSmul}.
Let $c > 0$ in $\bR$ and take a derivable vector $v$ in $C$ at $p$. 
We need to show that $c \cdot v$ is derivable. 
Take $\gamma \colon [0, \varepsilon) \to E$ such that it is differentiable at $0$ with $\gamma ([0, \varepsilon)) \subseteq C$, $\gamma (0) = p$ and $\gamma'(0) = v$. 
We want to show that
\begin{equation*}
\mu \colon \Bigl[0, \frac{\varepsilon}{c}\Bigr) \to E, x \mapsto \gamma (c \cdot x)
\end{equation*}
witnesses that $c \cdot v$ is derivable. 
We see that $\mu$ is derivable at $0$ and 
\begin{equation*}
\mu\Bigl(\Bigl[0, \frac{\varepsilon}{c}\Bigr)\Bigr) = \gamma ([0, \varepsilon)) \subseteq C.
\end{equation*}
Additionally, $\mu (0) = \gamma (c \cdot 0) = p$ and $\mu'(0) = c \cdot \gamma' (0) = c \cdot v$. 
Thus $c \cdot v$ is derivable. 
\end{proof}

The next lemma we need is the following for which the proof is inspired by \cite{Rios2018}:

\begin{lem}
Let $p \in C$. 
Then the derivable cone to $C$ at $p$ is closed in $E$. 
\end{lem}
\begin{proof}
We show that the derivable cone is sequentially closed. 
So let $v \colon \bN \to E$ be a sequence of derivable vectors within $v$ at $p$ that converges to $w \in E$. 
We need to show that $w$ is also derivable within $C$ at $p$.
For every $n \in \bN$ pick $\gamma_n \colon [0, \varepsilon_n) \to E$ such that for all $n \in \bN$ we have that $\gamma_n$ is differentiable at $0$, $\gamma_n ([0, \varepsilon_n)) \subseteq C$, $\gamma_n(0) = p$ and $\gamma_n'(0) = v_n$.    

We now need to define a map $\mu$ that witnesses that $w$ is derivable within $C$ at $p$. 
We want to construct this curve by combining parts of the curves $\gamma_n$.
We now need to describe which parts of the curve we want to pick.  

We first show the following: 

\begin{claim}\label{claim:existConvergence}
For all $n \in \bN$ there is
\begin{equation*}
i \in \Bigl(0, \frac{1}{n + 1}\Bigr]
\end{equation*}
such that for all $j \in (0, i]$, 
\begin{equation*}
\norm{\frac{\gamma_n(j) - p}{j} - v_n} < \frac{1}{n + 1}.
\end{equation*}
\begin{proof}
Because $\gamma_n'(0) = v_n$, we know that 
\begin{equation*}
\lim_{j \searrow 0}\norm{\frac{\gamma_n(j) - p}{j} - v_n} = 0
\end{equation*}
and thus such a $j$ must exist.
\end{proof}
\end{claim}


For every $n$, we pick an $i_n$ such that it fulfils the properties described in Claim \ref{claim:existConvergence} and additionally is smaller that $\varepsilon_n$.

Now we use these to define a decreasing sequence recursively:

\begin{equation*}
\iota \colon \bN \to \bR, n \mapsto \begin{cases}
    i_0 & n = 0 \\ 
    \min (\frac{i_{n-1}}{2}, i_n) & \text{otherwise} \\ 
\end{cases}
\end{equation*}

We collect some properties of this sequence: 

\begin{claim} The following hold for the sequence $\iota$. \label{claim:sequenceProps}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item For all $n \in \bN$, we have $\iota_n \in \bigl(0, \frac{1}{n + 1}\bigr]$. 
    \item For all $n \in \bN$, we know $\iota_n < \varepsilon_n$
    \item \label{point:normLT} For all $n \in \bN$ and $j \in (0, \iota_n]$, we have that 
        \begin{equation*}
            \norm{\frac{\gamma_n(j) - p}{j} - v_n} < \frac{1}{n + 1}.
        \end{equation*} 
    \item The sequence $\iota$ is antitone. 
    \item $\iota$ converges to $0$. 
\end{enumerate}
\begin{proof}
All of these follow directly from the definitions.
\end{proof}
\end{claim}

We can now use this to define the required curve: 

\begin{equation*}
\gamma \colon [0, \iota_0) \to E, x \mapsto \begin{cases} \gamma_n (x) & x \in (\iota_{n + 1}, \iota_n].\\ 
p & x = 0\\
\end{cases}
\end{equation*}

Now we can prove that this curve meets the required conditions: 

First, we get $\gamma(0) = p$ and for $x \in (\iota_{n + 1}, \iota_n]$, we have $x \le \iota_n < \varepsilon_n$ which means that $\gamma(x) = \gamma_n{x}$ is well-defined and therefore $\gamma(x) \in C$.
Therefore $\gamma([0, \iota_0)) \subseteq C$. 

Lastly, we need to show that $\gamma$ is differentiable at $0$ with derivative $w$.
We want to show that the difference quotient converges to $w$.
Let $\varepsilon > 0$. 
We pick an $N$ such that for all $n \ge N$
\begin{equation*}
    \norm{v_n - w} < \frac{\varepsilon}{2}    
\end{equation*}
and 
\begin{equation*}
    \frac{1}{N + 1} < \frac{\varepsilon}{2}.
\end{equation*}

We now want to show that for all $h \in (0, \iota_N)$ we get 

\begin{equation*}
    \norm{\frac{\gamma (h) - p}{h} - w} < \varepsilon.  
\end{equation*}

So let $h \in (0, \iota_N)$ and let $n \in \bN$ such that $h \in (\iota_{n + 1}, \iota_n]$. 

Then we have 

\begin{equation*}
\begin{split}
\norm{\frac{\gamma (h) - p}{h} - w} &= \norm{\frac{\gamma_n (h) - p}{h} - w} \\
&\le \norm{\frac{\gamma_n (h) - p}{h} - v_n} + \norm{v_n - w} \\
&\stackrel{(1)}{<} \norm{\frac{\gamma_n (h) - p}{h} - v_n} + \frac{\varepsilon}{2} \\
&\stackrel{(2)}{<} \frac{1}{n + 1} + \frac{\varepsilon}{2} \\
&\stackrel{(3)}{\le} \frac{1}{N + 1} + \frac{\varepsilon}{2} \\
&< \frac{\varepsilon}{2} + \frac{\varepsilon}{2} \\
&= \varepsilon \\
\end{split}
\end{equation*}

where in (1) and (3) we used that $n \ge N$ since $\iota$ is antitone.
In (2) we used property \ref{point:normLT} of Claim \ref{claim:sequenceProps}. 

\end{proof}

Now we need yet another cone: 

\begin{defi}
The set of all vectors $v$ such that there is an $r > 0$ such that $r \cdot v + p \in C$ is called the \emph{feasible cone} to $C$ at $p$.
\end{defi}

Again we get the following lemma: 

\begin{lem}
Let $C$ be convex. Then the feasible cone to $C$ at $p$ is a convex cone. 
\end{lem}

Now we can show our first equality of cones: 

\begin{lem}
Let $C$ be convex and $p \in C$. Then the tangent cone to $C$ at $p$ agrees with the closure of the feasible cone to $C$ at $p$. 
\end{lem}
