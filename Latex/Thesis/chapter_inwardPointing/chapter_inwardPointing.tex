\chapter{Inward Pointing Vectors in Manifolds with Corners}

Defining the notion of inward pointing vectors in manifolds with corners is substantially harder than in manifolds with boundary without corners. Let us first examine why this is. 

Here is a common definition for manifolds with boundary without corners:

\begin{defi}[\cite{Lee2006} p.\ 136]\label{defi:InwLee1}
    Let $M$ be a smooth manifold with boundary without corners. Let $p \in M$. Then $v \in \tanspace{p}{M} \setminus \tanspace{p}{\boundary M}$ is inward pointing if and only if there is an $\varepsilon > 0$ and a smooth curve $\gamma \colon [0, \varepsilon) \to M$ such that $\gamma (0) = p$ and $\gamma' (0) = v$.
\end{defi}

This definition cannot be used for manifolds with corners as in this case $\boundary M$ is not always a manifold and thus $\tanspace{p}{\boundary M}$ is not defined. 

The existence of the $\gamma \colon : [0, \varepsilon) \to M$ by itself characterises whether a vector is inward pointing or tangent to the boundary, a notion which we will call \emph{realisable}. 

So for manifolds with corners we either need a completely new definition that intrinsically excludes the vectors tangent to the boundary or we need a definition of realisability and a way to exclude the tangent vectors. 

\section{Definitions intrinsically excluding vectors tangent to boundary}

We first tried the first option and considered these candidates for definitions which we either came up with ourselves or found in the literature: 

\begin{defi}[Our idea inspired by Definition \ref{defi:InwLee1}]
    Let $M$ be a smooth manifold with corners. 
    Let $p \in M$. Then $v \in \tanspace{p}{M}$ is inward pointing if and only if there is an $\varepsilon > 0$ and a smooth curve $\gamma \colon [0, \varepsilon) \to M$ such that $\gamma (0) = p$, $\gamma' (0) = v$ and $\gamma ((0, \varepsilon)) \subseteq \interior{M}$.
\end{defi}

Unfortunately, this definition doesn't exclude vectors tangent to the boundary: 

\begin{example}\label{ex:InwLee2}
    Take $M$ to be the upper half plane $\mathcal{H}$ in $\bR^2$ let $p$ be $(0, 0)$ and let $\gamma \colon [0, 1) \to \mathcal{H}, x \mapsto x^2$. 
    Then $\gamma'(0) = (1, 0)$ and thus by this definition $(1, 0)$ would be inward pointing even though it is clearly tangent to the boundary.
    See Figure \ref{fig:InwLee2} for an illustration of this example. 
\end{example}

\begin{defi}[\cite{Melrose1996} Definition 1.13.10]\label{defi:InwMelrose}
    Let $M$ be a smooth manifold with corners. Let $p \in M$. Then $v \in \tanspace{p}{M}$ is inward pointing if and only if $\direcderiv{v}f (p) \ge 0$ for every non-negative $f \in \smoothfunc{M}$ that is zero on $\boundary{M}$. 
\end{defi}

For some manifolds this definition includes vectors tangent to the boundary: 

\begin{example}
    Take $M$ to be the upper half plane $\mathcal{H}$ in $\bR^2$ and let $p$ be $(0, 0)$. 
    Let $f$ be any non-negative function in $\smoothfunc{\mathcal{H}}$ that is zero on $\boundary{\mathcal{H}}$. 
    Then $\direcderiv{(0, 1)}f (0, 0) = 0$. 
    Thus $(0, 1)$ is inward pointing by this definition even though it is clearly tangent to the boundary. 
\end{example}

To remedy this we came up with this definition: 

\begin{defi}[Our idea inspired by Definition \ref{defi:InwMelrose}]\label{defi:InwMelrose2}
    Let $M$ be a smooth manifold with corners. Let $p \in M$. Then $v \in \tanspace{p}{M}$ is inward pointing if and only if there is a non-negative $f \in \smoothfunc{M}$ that is zero on $\boundary{M}$ such that $\direcderiv{v}f (p) > 0$. 
\end{defi}

Unfortunately, both of the above definitions do not work for manifolds with corners: 

\begin{example}
    Take $M$ to be the right upper quadrant $Q_I$ and let $p$ be $(0, 0)$. 
    Let $f$ be any non-negative function in $\smoothfunc{Q_I}$ that is zero on $\boundary{\mathcal{Q_I}}$. 
    Then clearly $\direcderiv{(0, 1)}f (0, 0) =\direcderiv{(1, 0)}f (0, 0) = 0$. 
    This means that the Jacobian is zero and since $f$ is smooth and thus differentiable at $(0, 0)$, this means that $\direcderiv{v}f (0, 0) = 0$ for all vectors $v$. 

    So in the case of Definition \ref{defi:InwMelrose}, this would mean that any vector was inward pointing, and in the case of Definition \ref{defi:InwMelrose2}, no vector would be inward pointing. 
\end{example}

Lastly, here is another popular definition that falls into this category: 

\begin{defi}[E.g.\ \cite{Joyce2009} Definition 2.2]
    Let $M$ be a smooth manifold with corners. 
    Let $p \in M$ be a corner point of codimension $k$, i.e.\ let there be a chart $(U, \varphi)$ with $U \subseteq \bR^n_k = [0, \infty)^k \times \bR^{n - k}$ such that $0 \in U$ and $\varphi(U) = p$. 
    Then a vector $v \in \tanspace{p}{M}$ is inward pointing if and only if it is in $\derivat{\varphi}{0} ((0, \infty)^k \times \bR^{n - k})$ where $\derivat{\varphi}{0} \colon \bR^n = \tanspace{0}{\bR^n_k} \to \tanspace{p}{M}$.
\end{defi}

This definition is of course correct, however it depends on a choice of chart.

\section{Definitions through realisability}

After failing to find a usable definition intrinsically excluding vectors, we moved on to defining realisable vectors.
Where as mentioned above, we want a realisable vector to be one that is either inward pointing or tangent to the boundary. 

Firstly, we could only find the following way to define inward pointing vectors from realisable ones: 

\begin{defi}[\cite{Amr2021}]
    Let $M$ be a smooth manifold with corners. Let $p \in M$. Then $v \in \tanspace{p}{M}$ is inward pointing if and only if it is in the interior of the set of realisable vectors. 
\end{defi}

Now we can move on to finding a definition of realisability. 

We first note conditions for such a definition: 

\begin{rem}\label{rem:realcond}
    A sensible definition of realisability needs to fulfil these two conditions: 
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item The definition needs to be preserved by local diffeomorphisms (in particular it should be preserved by smooth charts). \label{rem:realcond1}
        \item The definition should reduce to a sensible notion on the model space that is easily verifiable in the cases of the typical examples (e.g.\ upper half space in $\bR^n$). \label{rem:realcond2}
    \end{enumerate}
\end{rem}

Let us first discuss what this ``sensible notion'' in Remark \ref{rem:realcond2} should be. 

We first need the following definition:

\begin{defi}[Contingent Tangent Cone, \cite{Aubin1990} after Definition 4.1.1]
    Let $E$ be a normed vector space, $K \subset E$ and $x \in \closure{E}$. 
    Then the \emph{contingent tangent cone} to $K$ at $x$ is the set of all $v \in E$ such that there is a sequence of scalars $c \colon \bN \to \bR_\ge$ and a sequence of vectors $d \colon \bN \to E$ such that 
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item $\lim_{n \to \infty} d_n = 0$, 
        \item $x + d_n \in K$ for all $n \in \bN$, and 
        \item $\lim_{n \to \infty} c_n \cdot d_n = v$.
    \end{enumerate}
    We denote this tangent cone by $\tancone{K}{x}$. 
\end{defi}

In \mathlib this is referred to as \lstinline|posTangentConeAt|.
In the following we will only consider contingent tangent cones to convex sets for which the contingent tangent cone agrees with other notions of tangent cones such as the Clarke tangent cone (\cite{Aubin1990} Proposition 4.2.1).
Thus we will refer to it simply as the tangent cone. 

This captures exactly our notion of realisable: intuitively, the tangent cone $\tancone{K}{x}$ is composed of all vectors at $x$ that are either inward pointing or tangent to $K$. 
You can see examples of tangent cones in Figure \ref{fig:tancone}.

So we can take the ``sensible notion'' in Remark \ref{rem:realcond2} to mean that a vector $v$ should be inward pointing to a point $x$ in the model space if and only if $v$ is in the tangent cone at the point $x$ to the model space. 

Now let us consider possibilities for the definition of realisability.
The first was already provided to us in Definition \ref{defi:InwLee1}: 

\begin{defi}[\cite{Lee2006} p.\ 136]
    Let $M$ be a smooth manifold with corners. Let $p \in M$. Then $v \in \tanspace{p}{M}$ is \emph{realisable} if and only if there is an $\varepsilon > 0$ and a smooth curve $\gamma \colon [0, \varepsilon) \to M$ such that $\gamma (0) = p$ and $\gamma' (0) = v$.
\end{defi}

It is easy to show that this definition fulfils Remark \ref{rem:realcond1}. 
It also fulfils Remark \ref{rem:realcond2} but this is surprisingly hard to show for general model spaces $H$. 
Taking a vector tangent to the boundary, it is hard to describe a method for picking a smooth curve $\gamma \colon [0, \varepsilon) \to H$ such that $\gamma (0) = p$ and $\gamma' (0) = v$.
